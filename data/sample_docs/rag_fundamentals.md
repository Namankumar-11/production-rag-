Retrieval-Augmented Generation, or RAG, combines a retrieval system with a generative language model. Instead of relying purely on knowledge baked into the model's weights during training, RAG fetches relevant documents at query time and feeds them into the model's context window. This allows the system to answer questions about private or recent information the model was never trained on.

Chunking is important in a RAG system because language models have limited context windows and retrieval systems work best over small, focused units of text. If documents are indexed whole, a search for one fact pulls in a huge amount of irrelevant surrounding text, wasting context budget and diluting relevance signals. Good chunking splits documents on natural boundaries like sentences or sections rather than arbitrary character counts, and typically includes a small overlap between adjacent chunks so that facts near a chunk boundary are not orphaned from their context.

Hallucination is one of the biggest risks in RAG systems: a language model can generate a fluent, confident-sounding answer that is not actually supported by the retrieved context. Hallucination is detected in generated answers by verifying that every claim in the answer is grounded in the source material it cites. One lightweight approach computes lexical overlap between each sentence and the chunk it claims to be citing; low overlap signals the citation may be fabricated or the claim may be unsupported. A more expensive but more accurate approach uses a second LLM call as a judge to assess semantic entailment between the claim and the source.

Evaluation is what separates a production RAG system from a prototype. A labeled evaluation set of queries paired with known-relevant documents allows a team to compute retrieval metrics like recall, precision, and mean reciprocal rank every time the pipeline changes, catching regressions before they reach users.
